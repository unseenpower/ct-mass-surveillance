The Task Force Exception

Five Connecticut towns' Flock audit logs record 12.9 million searches against their cameras. Only 3% came from Connecticut. Among the rest are all four regional RISS centers, nine fusion centers and nineteen federal agencies — none of which any Connecticut town has ever discussed in a public meeting. On 1 October a new state law draws a fence around this. The fence has a gate in it, and the gate is a term the statute never defines.

The first thing the data settles is that "sharing" is the wrong mental model. A Connecticut town does not hand its footage to a partner. It leaves its cameras inside a national pool, and an officer anywhere in the country runs one query against several thousand networks at once.

The median search touching a Connecticut camera in this period spanned thousands of networks. Nearly nine in ten spanned more than a thousand. That is why a Missouri drug task force appears in Manchester's audit log thousands of times without anyone in Missouri ever deciding to look at Manchester: Manchester is simply inside the default.

It also means the raw counts below understate nothing and overstate nothing about intent. These are not targeted intrusions. They are the ordinary consequence of a design in which the search box defaults to the whole country.

The New England node

NESPIN — the New England State Police Information Network — ran 5,363 searches that reached a Connecticut town in this window, across all five towns, from January 2023 to the last day of our data. Its average search spanned 5,488 camera networks.

Set against USA TODAY's national Flock corpus, that figure has a striking property: NESPIN ran 12,236 searches nationwide in the same period, so 43.8% of everything NESPIN has ever searched in Flock touched one of five Connecticut towns. Not because NESPIN is interested in Farmington, but because its searches are national by default and Farmington is in the pool.

NESPIN matters more than its volume, because of what it is. It is one of six centers in the federally funded Regional Information Sharing Systems program, and it is the hinge between Connecticut and the rest of RISSNET. Connecticut's own fusion centre, CTIC, is connected to RISSNET through NESPIN.

And NESPIN is not a police department. It is New England State Police Information Network, Inc., a 501(c)(3) charitable corporation, EIN 04-3154163, at 124 Grove Street, Franklin, Massachusetts, founded 1992, roughly $4.5m in annual revenue, operating the RISS program under a Department of Justice award. That legal form is about to matter a great deal.

What the law actually says

Connecticut's ALPR statute — Public Act 26-14, from House Bill 5449 — takes effect for these purposes on 1 October 2026. Its core move is to replace a national default with a closed list of permitted recipients. Section 1(c)(6) prohibits sharing ALPR data unless the requester is:

(A) a public agency or law enforcement agency of this state, (B) a law enforcement agency of the state of New York or Rhode Island or the Commonwealth of Massachusetts or a municipality of said states or commonwealth or a multijurisdictional task force of which a public agency or law enforcement agency of this state is a participating member … (C) any other law enforcement agency … including any federal law enforcement agency, if such requesting law enforcement agency has a judicially issued probable cause warrant for the specific data requested, or is requesting specific data on an individual identified as a possible match in the Federal Terrorist Screening Database … PA 26-14 § 1(c)(6)

Measured against the graph above, this is a demolition order. Texas agencies alone ran 2.86 million searches into these five towns; Georgia 1.53 million; Florida 1.12 million. None of those states is on the list. Neither is Missouri, home of MOCIC, nor Tennessee, home of ROCIC. On 1 October, the overwhelming majority of the traffic in that visualisation becomes unlawful to serve.

Three gaps worth naming

1. "Multijurisdictional task force" is never defined. The act defines "law enforcement agency", "ALPR data", "hotlist" and "gender-affirming health care services." It does not define the one term that decides whether a regional intelligence network gets in. NESPIN is not a task force in any ordinary sense — it is a standing information-sharing utility with roughly 973 member agencies across New England — but it is exactly the sort of body that will be argued into the phrase, because nothing in the text forecloses it.

2. NESPIN is not a Massachusetts law enforcement agency. Subparagraph (B) reaches "a law enforcement agency of … the Commonwealth of Massachusetts or a municipality of said … commonwealth." NESPIN, Inc. is a private 501(c)(3) charitable corporation. It is not an agency of the Commonwealth, not a municipality, and not a federal agency. Read strictly, it fits nowhere in (A)–(D) — which makes the undefined phrase in (B) the only door it can come through, and makes how that phrase gets construed the single highest-leverage question in the statute.

3. The Terrorist Screening Database is a warrantless federal path. Subparagraph (C) lets any federal agency obtain data without a warrant on "an individual identified as a possible match in the Federal Terrorist Screening Database." A watchlist "possible match" is a low bar, it is not judicially reviewed, and the statute attaches no audit obligation specific to it.

What the act does well

Three provisions are stronger than the coverage suggested, and they are the ones to build on.

  • § 1(c)(7) — the network condition. A Connecticut agency may not participate in any multistate or national data-sharing network unless the network itself requires every participant to sign a compliance declaration as a condition of access. Flock's national network does not do this. Neither does RISSNET. Read plainly, this does not merely restrict who Connecticut shares with — it makes continued participation in the national pool, as presently constituted, unlawful.
  • § 1(c)(8) — no standing feeds. No public agency may have "real-time, bulk or automatic access" except in response to a documented, case-specific request. Fusion-centre integration is, definitionally, a standing feed. Fairfield's own police report describes "unfettered access to the Bridgeport Fusion Center and partnering federal agencies" — the exact arrangement this provision forbids.
  • § 1(d) — the audit logs stay public. ALPR data itself is now exempt from FOIA. But "any data … derived from any audit of an automated license plate reader system, usage logs for such system and logs detailing access to" that data remains disclosable, with plate data redacted. That is precisely the record this entire analysis is built from, and the legislature has now written it into statute.
  • § 1(g) — a private right of action. From 1 October, any aggrieved individual may sue for injunctive or declaratory relief, "including a determination of past violations," in the superior court where they live, with costs and reasonable attorney's fees on success, and the case is privileged for trial assignment. No damages — but a court can be asked to declare that past sharing was unlawful.
  • § 3 — mandatory disclosure of the graph. Every agency must report annually, by 31 January, "times automated license plate reader data was shared with or accessed by another entity, the identity of each of those entities and the reason for sharing the data," publish it on its own website, and file it with UConn's Institute for Municipal and Regional Policy, which submits a consolidated report to the Governor and the Public Safety and Judiciary committees by 30 July 2027.

That last one is the sleeper. The state is about to compel every Connecticut department to publish the same graph this page reconstructs from FOIA. The first reports are due 31 January 2027, covering calendar 2026 — a year already half-documented here. Which makes the audit data a check on those reports rather than a substitute for them.

One caution on the timetable: the POSTC model policy is due 1 December 2026 and agencies must adopt it by 1 January 2027, but binding DESPP regulations are not required until 1 January 2033. Between the two sits six years of policy-not-regulation.

Where the oversight isn't

Connecticut's fusion centre, CTIC, has no enabling statute. It exists under the general emergency-management authority of DESPP's Division of Emergency Management and Homeland Security, Title 28, chapter 517. Its own state web page names a director and a partner list — DHS, FBI, TSA, HIDTA, the National Guard, Corrections, the Judicial Branch, State Police, local departments — and cites no statute, names no oversight body, and links no privacy policy. There is no legislative reporting requirement, no inspector general, no sunset.

NESPIN is further out of reach again. As a private nonprofit corporation it is not a Connecticut public agency, so the state Freedom of Information Act does not reach it; it is not a federal agency, so the federal FOIA does not either. It is funded by Department of Justice awards and governed by its own board.

The one federal rule that does govern this world is 28 CFR Part 23, the operating policy for federally funded multijurisdictional criminal intelligence systems, which requires reasonable suspicion of criminal conduct before information about a person is entered. Two things limit it badly here. It governs criminal intelligence records, not query access to someone else's camera network. And RISS's own published clarification places exactly this class of data outside the definition:

… such sources as the Internet, newspapers, motor vehicle administration records, and other public record information on-line, such sources shall not be considered part of criminal intelligence systems. RISS, 28 CFR Part 23 Policy Clarification

Part 23 also carries no private right of action; it is enforced, if at all, as a condition on federal funding. So the sole federal regime over fusion and RISS intelligence sharing arguably does not reach ALPR queries at all — and Connecticut's own law, which does, has never once been tested against these entities.

He has found names, IP addresses, as well as a Facebook page, and now has turned it over to the NESPIN, which is basically the cyber crimes unit for the Connecticut State Police. North Canaan Board of Selectmen, 4 May 2026

It is not that. It is a Massachusetts charity running a federal information-sharing program across six states and into RISSNET. The single time in 31,089 meetings that a Connecticut elected official says the name of the entity that ran 5,363 searches into Connecticut cameras, they describe it as a unit of their own state police.

The substantive fusion-centre discussions that do exist are worth reading, and they are not about ALPR. Danbury held an entire ad-hoc meeting in January 2021 on a grant whose condition was that "the city of Danbury shall provide a full-time qualified law enforcement officer as a regional intelligence liaison officer with the CTIC fusion center … for a period of two years" — the actual mechanism by which a Connecticut town joins the fusion centre. Hartford's council in September 2018 debated a $60,000 fusion-centre reimbursement and an alder asked what it meant "for the undocumented community" — the question PA 26-14 finally answered eight years later.

What this opens

  • The construction of "multijurisdictional task force" is the whole ballgame. POSTC writes the model policy by 1 December 2026. If that policy defines the term to exclude standing information-sharing networks such as RISS centres and fusion centres, the fence holds. If it is silent, the gate stays open by default. There are two POSTC meetings left before the deadline.
  • The § 1(c)(7) network condition is the strongest untested provision in the act. It is not a restriction on recipients; it is a condition on participation. Whether Flock's national network can satisfy it — and what a Connecticut department is supposed to do on 1 October if it cannot — appears to be unasked so far.
  • Ask the towns directly, before the reports are due. The § 3 annual report must name every entity data was shared with. A FOIA now for the current network-sharing configuration and the Flock event log — the only export that records who toggled which sharing setting — establishes the baseline that the January 2027 report can be checked against.
  • CTIC itself is unexamined. No statute, no published privacy policy, no legislative reporting. Whether CTIC holds a Flock account, queries through NESPIN, or receives ALPR data from member towns is not answerable from the data here — CTIC appears nowhere in 5,646 searching agencies — and is a clean FOIA question to DESPP.
  • Fairfield's "unfettered access" language deserves a follow-up. It is a town's own written description of a standing feed to a fusion centre, published before the statute that forbids one.
